# Seeing the Source: Reductionism, Perception, and Identity

Thomas Lee Abshier, ND · Isak Gutierrez | 12 October 2026

*From a morning discussion that began with the problem of categorizing Thomas's interdisciplinary essays and moved into CPP philosophy, perception, and the nature of identity.*

---

## 1. Every Vector Ends at God

Isak described sitting in his backyard watching light pass through hanging glass light-catchers. The light was redistributed in many directions without losing its power. He then looked at a bush and broke down what was happening: the light reaching his eyes is sunlight; the bush redirects it to him. A person standing next to him receives different photons at a slightly different angle — same source, same object, different photons.

He extended this to a spiritual parallel: suppose there is a Source sending out not only photons but something arriving from every direction at once and leaving in every direction at once. In CPP terms, that constant motion still allows clusters of particles to "decide" to behave together, forming DNA, following its rules, and repeating them through death and new birth.

> "I'm not looking at a bush. I'm looking *toward* a bush. The only reason I'm seeing it is because something else is showing it to me."

Thomas characterized this as a **reductionist track**: pick any vector, follow it down — the bush, to photons, to conscious points, to God — and you arrive at God. His whole project has been showing that you can find God everywhere you look. That one can go in any direction and find God at the end of it is itself an indication that God underlies everything.

## 2. Sensation Implies an Observer

Thomas turned to another feature of life: it has sensation — hot and cold, red and blue, pain and pleasure. Each sensation produces a particular pattern of brain activation. But to perceive a configuration there must be a perceiver. So the observer — the "I" — has a conscious-point nature of its own.

He was careful: he does not know whether the observer *is* a conscious point, but there is certainly a conscious-point nature associated with our perception of pain, heat, color, and the rest.

## 3. The Conscious Entity

Each pattern — conscious points configured with nerves, configured in turn with more conscious points — has an identity of its own. When a sufficient critical mass of consciousness is oriented in a certain way, it forms its own pattern and becomes its own **conscious entity**.

There is a beingness associated with any concentration of conscious points together with the diffuse space around it. Space plus the entity together give distinction; that distinction is made of particular conscious points; it has some degree of awareness; and it stands in relationship to other things.

## 4. Identity Depends on the Frame the Observer Brings

A rock emerges in a canyon, from under a glacier, or out of a volcano. In itself it is just an "it" — until someone comes along and says, "This will make a great doorstop." Now it has the identity of a doorstop.

Another person picks up the same collection of conscious points and says, "This has a fossil in it. It needs to be cut — it's a lapidary project, it belongs in a museum." Same object, different identity, because the two people brought different frames to it.

> Identity depends a great deal on what we bring. If we bring nothing, we likely do not notice the thing at all.

Thomas tied this back to the starting problem: categorizing a body of work is itself an identity problem — what a thing *is* depends on what the observer brings to it.
