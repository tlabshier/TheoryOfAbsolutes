**Copilot:** That’s a deep and fascinating question—and it touches the heart of quantum weirdness.


In short: we can’t directly determine the transverse width of a single photon just by the dimensions of the slits in a double-slit experiment. But the interference pattern and diffraction effects do give us indirect clues about the spatial coherence and wave-like extent of the photon’s probability amplitude.


Here’s why:


🌊 Photons as Quantum Wavepackets

A photon isn’t a tiny billiard ball—it’s better described as a quantum wavepacket with a certain spatial extent. In the double-slit setup:


- If the slit separation is smaller than the transverse coherence width of the photon’s wavefunction, you get interference.

- If the slits are too far apart, the photon behaves as if it only goes through one slit—no interference.

So, the ability to produce an interference pattern tells us that the photon’s wavefunction must be wide enough to “cover” both slits simultaneously.


🧠 But What About the “Size” of a Photon?

This is where it gets tricky. The photon doesn’t have a well-defined size in the classical sense. Its transverse extent depends on:


- The source (e.g., laser vs. spontaneous emission)

- The optical setup (collimation, apertures, etc.)

- The coherence properties of the light

 In practice, the slit width and separation act as a kind of “ruler” for the transverse coherence of the photon field—but not for the photon’s literal size.


🧪 Experimental Insight

In single-photon double-slit experiments, the interference pattern builds up over time, even when photons are sent one at a time. This suggests that each photon interferes with itself, and its wavefunction must span both slits. But the pattern’s fringe spacing is determined by the wavelength, not the photon’s “width.”


So while the slit geometry constrains what kind of interference you can observe, it doesn’t pin down a hard boundary for the photon’s transverse size. It’s more accurate to say the slits probe the coherence and spatial structure of the photon’s wavefunction.

🌌 Coherence and Spatial Structure of a Single-Photon Wavefunction

A single photon emitted from a quantum dot typically has a wavefunction that is spatially extended, especially in the transverse direction. This wavefunction isn’t a sharply bounded object—it’s a probability amplitude that decays smoothly, often modeled as a Gaussian beam or a wavepacket with finite spatial coherence.

The transverse coherence width (or spatial coherence length) of the photon’s wavefunction determines whether it can “cover” both slits and produce interference. This coherence width depends on:


- The emission aperture and optical setup (e.g. collimation, lenses)

- The angular divergence of the photon beam

- The source size and emission mode of the quantum dot


📏 What Slit Dimensions Still Produce Interference at 650 nm?

Let’s assume you’re using single photons at 650 nm from a well-collimated quantum dot source. Based on experimental data and coherence theory:


- Slit separation (center-to-center):

Interference is typically observed for separations up to 10–50 microns, depending on the beam’s transverse coherence. Beyond that, the photon’s wavefunction may not span both slits coherently.

- Slit width:

Usually 1–10 microns. Narrower slits increase diffraction and fringe visibility but reduce photon throughput.

- Inner barrier (between slits):

Often 1–20 microns. This is essentially the gap that the wavefunction must “bridge” to interfere with itself.

- Outer extent (total slit array width):

Could be 20–100 microns or more, but only the coherent overlap region matters for interference.

So, for a 650 nm photon, a double-slit with 5 μm wide slits, 10 μm separation, and total width ~20 μm would be well within the range to observe interference—assuming good spatial coherence.


🧠 What This Tells Us About the Photon’s Wavefunction

If interference is observed, the photon’s transverse wavefunction must have non-negligible amplitude across both slits. That implies a transverse coherence width at least as large as the slit separation—say, 10–20 microns in this case.

But this doesn’t mean the photon “has a size” of 20 microns. Rather, its probability amplitude is spread over that region, and the interference pattern is a manifestation of that spread.

### **1. Transverse Coherence Width: What Is It?**


The **transverse coherence width** is a measure of the spatial extent over which the photon’s wavefunction maintains its coherence. In simpler terms:


- It describes the region where the **phase relationship** of the wavefunction is consistent enough for interference effects to occur.

- For a single photon, it’s the effective width of the **probability amplitude** in the transverse plane.


The coherence width depends on:


- **The angular divergence** of the light source.

- **The spatial size** of the emission aperture or source.

- **The wavelength** of the light.


---


### **2. Key Parameters and Formulas**


#### **a. Angular Divergence (\( \theta \))**


The angular divergence of the light beam determines how much the wavefront spreads as it propagates. It is related to the source size and wavelength.

For a Gaussian beam, the angular divergence is given by:

$$ \theta = \frac{\lambda}{\pi w_0} $$

Where:

– \( \lambda \): Wavelength of the light

– \( w_0 \): Beam waist, the narrowest part of the beam (related to the source size)


#### **b. Transverse Coherence Width (\( L_c )\)**


The transverse coherence width is inversely related to the angular divergence:

$$ L_c = \frac{\lambda}{\Delta \theta} $$

Where:

– \( \Delta \theta \): Angular spread of the beam (similar to \( \theta \), but measured for the source)

– \( \lambda \): Wavelength of the light

If the source is collimated (low divergence), \( L_c \) becomes larger, meaning the photon’s wavefunction is spread over a broader transverse region.


#### **c. Spatial Coherence and Source Size**


For a light source with finite spatial size ( d ), the coherence width is related to the source size and distance ( z ) from the source:

$$ L_c \sim \frac{\lambda z}{d} $$

Where:

– \( d \): Effective size of the light source

– \( z \): Distance from the source to the plane of interest

– \( \lambda \): Wavelength of the photon


This equation shows that smaller sources (or better-collimated beams) produce larger coherence widths.

### **3. Example Calculation**


Let’s calculate the transverse coherence width for a **650 nm photon** emitted from a quantum dot source.


#### **Assumptions**


– Wavelength: \( \lambda = 650, \text{nm} = 6.5 \times 10^{-7}, \text{m} \)

– Emission aperture size: \( d = 1, \mu\text{m} = 1 \times 10^{-6}, \text{m} \)

– Distance to the double-slit plane: \( z = 1, \text{m} \)

Using the Formula

$$ L_c = \frac{\lambda z}{d} $$

Substituting values:

$$ L_c = \frac{(6.5 \times 10^{-7})(1)}{1 \times 10^{-6}} = 0.65, \text{m} $$


$$ L_c = 0.65, \text{m} $$


This result suggests that the transverse coherence width of the photon’s wavefunction can extend as much as **0.65 meters** in this setup, assuming perfect collimation. This is an idealized scenario, and practical factors like beam divergence and imperfect collimation would reduce this value.

### **4. Practical Implications**


#### **a. Double-Slit Interference**


For a double-slit experiment:

– The slit separation must be smaller than or comparable to the transverse coherence width \( L_c \) for interference to occur.

– If the slits are too far apart (e.g., greater than \( L_c \)), the photon’s wavefunction cannot coherently span both slits, and interference patterns will disappear.


#### **b. Source Engineering**


– To maximize \( L_c \), use a smaller source size (\( d \)) and better collimation optics to reduce the angular divergence (\( \Delta \theta )\).

– Quantum dot sources often emit light with high spatial coherence, making them suitable for interference experiments.


### **5. Insights from Coherence Theory**


#### **a. Gaussian Beam Approximation**


In many cases, the photon’s wavefunction can be modeled as a Gaussian beam, with a transverse intensity profile given by:

$$ I(r) = I_0 \exp\left(-\frac{2r^2}{w(z)^2}\right) $$

Where:

– \( r \): Radial distance from the beam center

– \( w(z) \): Beam width at distance ( z ), which expands with distance


#### **b. Effective Coherence Width**


The transverse coherence width can be approximated as the region within **1–2 standard deviations** of the beam’s intensity distribution. This captures the region where the probability amplitude is significant, even if it technically extends to infinity.

- In a double-slit experiment, the slit separation and geometry probe the **coherence properties** of the photon’s wavefunction, not its “size.”
