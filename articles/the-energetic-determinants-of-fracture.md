# The Energetic and Entropic Determinants of Fracture

**The Energetic and Entropic Determinants of Fracture ****
by Thomas Lee Abshier, ND, and Copilot,

6/21/2025


**Thomas: **When an object breaks, what is the principle that is being satisfied? Is it an increase of entropy by breaking into more possible configurations, or is it going into a lower energy state that is more favorable and probable?


**Copilot: **Great question, Thomas—this dives into some really elegant physics. When an object breaks, **both entropy and energy considerations** come into play, but which one dominates depends on the context.


1. **Entropy**


When something breaks into smaller pieces, it generally **increases the number of possible configurations** the system can take—this means higher entropy. Think of a coffee mug: as a whole, it has relatively few ways it can be arranged. When it shatters, there are exponentially more ways the shards can be distributed in space. So yes, breaking often increases entropy, which aligns with the second law of thermodynamics—the universe tends toward disorder.


2. **Energy**


At the same time, breaking can also lead to a **more stable, lower-energy state**, especially if the object was under internal stress or strain. For example, a stretched elastic band snapping or a brittle material cracking under pressure releases stored mechanical energy. The new state, although broken, might be more energetically favorable because that tension or stress is gone.


So which one “drives” the process?


Usually, it’s a blend: entropy gives it direction (more possible outcomes), and energy determines if it’s likely to happen spontaneously. If breaking leads to **both lower energy and higher entropy**, the process is highly favorable and probably inevitable.

This is a nuanced question that ties together **thermodynamics**, **material science**, and **statistical mechanics**. Let’s expand on the interplay between **entropy** and **energy** when an object breaks, and add some additional context to refine the discussion:


---


### **1. Entropy Considerations**


#### **What Happens to Entropy When an Object Breaks?**


- Entropy (\(S\)) is a measure of the number of possible microscopic configurations (or states) a system can take.

- When an object is whole, it has a relatively limited number of ways its molecules and atoms can be arranged while still maintaining the macroscopic structure. It is said to have a **low entropy state**.

- When the object breaks, it fragments into smaller pieces, and the system can now be rearranged in many more ways. This dramatic increase in the number of configurations corresponds to a large **increase in entropy**.


#### **Examples of Entropy Increase:**


- **Shattered Glass**:


A glass cup has a very specific macroscopic structure. When it shatters, the pieces can be distributed in numerous ways on the floor, drastically increasing the system’s entropy.


- **Chemical Bonds Breaking**:


In molecular systems, breaking chemical bonds (e.g., dissociation of molecules) often leads to more possible arrangements of the fragments, increasing entropy.


#### **Entropy as a Driving Force**:


- **The Second Law of Thermodynamics** states that the total entropy of a closed system (or the universe) tends to increase over time.

- This principle provides a “direction” for breaking processes: systems naturally evolve toward states of higher entropy, where there are more possible configurations.


---


### **2. Energy Considerations**


#### **What Happens to Energy When an Object Breaks?**


- The **energy** of a system determines how favorable a process is, particularly in terms of whether the system moves to a more stable, lower-energy state.

- When an object breaks, two main energy-related factors come into play:


**Release of Stored Energy**:


Breaking releases this stored potential energy if the object is under mechanical stress, strain, or tension. For example:


A stretched elastic band snapping.

- A brittle rock fracturing under compression.


- The release of stored energy often results in a **more stable, lower-energy state**.


- **Energy Required to Break Bonds**:


Breaking typically requires an energy input** to overcome the forces holding the object together (e.g., covalent bonds, Van der Waals forces, or mechanical cohesion). External forces (e.g., dropping the object, applying tension) often supply this energy.


#### **Key Balance of Energy**:


- If **breaking** releases more energy (from stress or strain) than it takes to overcome the bonds holding the object together, the process is energetically favorable and spontaneous.

- If breaking requires more energy than is released, it won’t happen spontaneously without external input.


---


### **3. Which Principle Dominates?**


- The **dominance of energy or entropy** depends on the system and conditions:


**Stress-Driven Breaking**:


In systems under high stress (e.g., a stretched rubber band or a compressed material), the release of stored energy dominates the breaking process.


- **Entropy-Driven Breaking**:


In systems where breaking leads to a large increase in entropy (e.g., shattering a brittle material), the increase in disorder plays a more dominant role.


- **Combined Effects**:


Both factors drive most real-world breaking processes:


Energy considerations determine whether the process is **spontaneous**.

- Entropy considerations determine the **direction** and **likelihood** of the resulting configurations.


#### **Thermodynamic Free Energy**:


- The principle that balances energy and entropy is the free energy, ( G ) (Gibbs free energy for systems at constant pressure):$$ \Delta G = \Delta H – T\Delta S $$Where:


**ΔG**: Change in free energy (must be negative for a spontaneous process).

- **ΔH**: Change in enthalpy (represents the energy change).

- **TΔS**: Change in entropy, scaled by absolute temperature ( T ).


A process is spontaneous when \( \Delta G < 0 \):


- If \( \Delta H < 0 \), the process is **energetically favorable**.

- If \( \Delta S > 0 \), the process is **entropically favorable**.


This equation beautifully captures the tug-of-war between energy minimization and entropy maximization in determining what nature “wants” to do.


### **4. Examples of Energy vs. Entropy Dominance**


#### **a. Breaking a Brittle Material (Shattering a Glass)**


- **Energy**: Breaking covalent or Van der Waals bonds in the material requires energy. However, if the material is under stress (e.g., dropped or impacted), that stress energy is released, driving the breakage.

- **Entropy**: The shattered pieces have many more possible positions and configurations than the intact glass, resulting in a large increase in entropy.


#### **b. Chemical Reactions**


- Breaking chemical bonds requires energy input, but the resulting fragments may have much higher entropy due to the increased number of configurations.

- For example:


The dissociation of water into hydrogen and oxygen gases significantly increases entropy, even though energy must be supplied to break the bonds.


#### **c. Rubber Band Snapping**


- **Energy**: A stretched rubber band stores elastic potential energy. When it snaps, this energy is released, driving the process.

- **Entropy**: The system’s entropy also increases as the rubber’s molecular chains become more disordered.


---


### **5. Edge Cases**


- **Entropy-Driven but Energy-Costly**:


Some processes increase entropy but require significant energy input. For example, melting ice increases entropy but requires heat energy to overcome hydrogen bonding.


- **Energy-Driven but Entropy-Decreasing**:


Some processes release energy but decrease entropy. For example, crystallization releases heat but leads to a more ordered state.


---


### **6. Conclusion**


When an object breaks:


- **Entropy** drives the process toward higher disorder, as breaking creates more possible configurations for the system.

- **Energy** considerations determine whether the process is spontaneous by assessing whether the system moves to a lower-energy state.


In most cases, **both principles act together**:


- The process is favored when **energy is released** (lower enthalpy) and **entropy increases** (higher disorder).

- The free energy, (\( G \)), captures the balance of energy and entropy, determining the breaking process’s spontaneity.
